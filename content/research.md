### Traffic assignment

Traffic assignment problems (TAPs) predict how travelers choose routes under the congestion that traffic itself creates. My research aims to make these problems more robust to errors in input data and faster to solve. This matters because TAPs are often solved thousands of times inside larger problems such as network design, origin-destination estimation, and model calibration.

TAPs are nonlinear optimization problems, so they cannot be solved exactly in finite time; instead, iterative procedures move closer and closer to equilibrium. As it turns out, an extremely fundamental question in traffic assignment, when to stop these iterative procedures, was not understood theoretically. We often stop at a relative gap of 10<sup>−4</sup>, but that threshold comes from empirical studies, and such rule-of-thumb measures say nothing direct about the error in flows or travel times when a TAP is terminated early. For logit-based stochastic user equilibrium (SUE), we derived **bounds on how far any solution can be from equilibrium** in path flows, link flows, link costs, and total system travel time, using only quantities that each iteration already computes. In a network design application, these bounds let us **discard poor designs after a few iterations** instead of solving each one fully. This work began as a master's thesis that received the **CUTC Milton Pikarsky Memorial Award**. [[See the full preprint here]](https://ssrn.com/abstract=7677338)

In another work, we studied the Jacobian of the logit mapping, the function that predicts users' route choices for fixed route costs. It turns out that **its eigenvalues are real and non-positive**, which explains why the method of successive averages converges linearly when its step size is small enough, and we derived a bound on how small the step size must be to guarantee this. The same analysis led to a **Newton method that converges quadratically** near equilibrium. Our open-source solver, Logit-SUE, solves the Chicago Regional network (about 39,000 links and 2.3 million origin-destination pairs) to a relative gap of 10<sup>−10</sup> **in about five minutes**. [[See a preprint of our work here]](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2605.21843) [[Try out the logit-SUE TAP solver!]](https://github.com/spartalab/logit-SUE)

I have also worked on dynamic traffic assignment with uncertain demand. We showed that a more complex model that captures queue spillback, such as the cell transmission model (CTM), can perform worse than a simpler model, such as a point queue model, when the input data is erroneous. Queue spillback makes models more realistic, but it also makes them more sensitive to errors in demand forecasts, which can push predicted flows far from reality. We developed a method that models **spillback only on links where it is likely to occur** and identifies those links algorithmically across many demand scenarios. On the Austin network, this approach was **more robust than full spillback** when demand errors were large. [[See a preprint of our work here]](mailto:debojjalb@utexas.edu?subject=Request%20for%20localized%20queue%20spillback%20preprint)

### Port and maritime logistics

We often look at road systems to model delays and routing behavior. However, a large chunk of the flow on roads often originates from other systems, such as maritime ports. When a port gets congested, the delays may not stay inside the port and can often spread to the roads and rail lines that serve it.

A port is a system of several interacting components: an anchorage where vessels wait, a navigation channel with several navigational restrictions, terminals where cargo is handled, and a landside interface of roadways, railways, and pipelines that moves cargo inland. Since many of these components behave like queueing systems, a bottleneck in one part of the system can have a disproportionate impact on many others. In a project with the U.S. Army Engineer Research and Development Center, we studied the Port of Houston, the largest U.S. port by waterborne tonnage, as **one integrated system** rather than as individual terminals.

Looking at vessel data from Houston, we noticed that right after a disruption, the port cleared vessels much faster for a while than it could sustain over the long run. This led us to separate **operating capacity**, the throughput a port can sustain over long periods, from **ultimate capacity**, the most it can process for a short time during a surge or after a disruption, and to develop system-level methods for estimating both. We estimated operating capacity with a parsimonious queueing model and ultimate capacity by fitting a differential equation model to simulation outputs. For Houston, we found that **liquid-bulk terminals limited stable operations**, while **pilot availability became the binding constraint after disruptions**. [[See a preprint of our work here]](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2509.22961)

To put these methods into practice, we developed **MAPS** (Multimodal Analysis Port Simulation), open-source software that lets planners test how new infrastructure would change capacity and resilience at a multimodal port. MAPS simulates a multi-terminal, multi-commodity port, and a channel scheduling algorithm links vessel transits to terminal operations. We used it to evaluate a hypothetical expansion of the Houston Ship Channel and found that the expansion **added little long-term capacity but sped up recovery after a hurricane**. This work received the **Best Poster Award** at the UT Austin Civil Engineering department's Graduate Symposium and **second prize** in the Texas Institute of Transportation Engineers Student Paper competition. [[See a preprint of our work here]](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.7447899) [[Try out MAPS!]](https://github.com/spartalab/port-simulation)

Underneath these models is a lot of data. Using **AIS vessel tracking data**, we characterized how vessels arrive and queue at Houston over time, including the queues that build up when fog closes the channel. [[Read the AIS data processing paper]](https://doi.org/10.1061/jwped5.wweng-2441) [[Read the fog analysis paper]](https://doi.org/10.1061/jwped5.wweng-2451)

We are now taking this work statewide through a project funded by the Texas Department of Transportation. Our team is building a **multimodal freight model for Texas spanning waterways, railways, and roadways**, along with a decision-support tool, built on stochastic traffic assignment, that predicts how freight moves across the state. The tool is meant to help evaluate infrastructure investments, prepare for disruptions, and compare future scenarios. More broadly, we are interested in resilience and re-routing during disruptions, data integration, and economic questions such as port pricing and demurrage.

### Trucking logistics

I am also interested in routing and scheduling problems in freight logistics, especially extensions of classic vehicle routing problems with multiple objectives and constraints such as delivery time windows, in contexts like last-mile delivery and electric freight vehicles.

With e-commerce growing and delivery fleets going electric, the shortest delivery route is not necessarily the best one. Because an electric vehicle recovers energy through **regenerative braking**, the energy a route uses depends not just on its length but also on the slopes along it. Left turns across oncoming traffic also create conflicts at intersections that make a route less safe. In a project at the Indian Institute of Science, we looked at how to route last-mile electric delivery vehicles to **use less energy while making fewer left turns**, while still meeting customers' delivery time windows.

Modeling turns was tricky, because whether a move is a left turn depends on three consecutive intersections rather than two. We handled this by building the model on the line graph of the road network, and formulated the problem as a bi-criterion Steiner traveling salesperson problem with time windows. We solved small instances exactly with mixed-integer programming and developed a local search heuristic for larger ones. We then tested the heuristic on **16 Amazon delivery routes in Austin, TX**, each with 80 to 209 delivery stops, where our model performed considerably better than an adapted version of the well-known Lin-Kernighan-Helsgaun (LKH) heuristic. [[The paper is published in Computers & Operations Research]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cor.2025.107286) [[See the code]](https://github.com/transnetlab/last-mile-ev-logistics)
